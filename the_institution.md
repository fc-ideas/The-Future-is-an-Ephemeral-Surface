# The Most Important AI Technology Might Be an Institution

**Fabrizio Costa**  | September 2026

The standard AI story is simple. Machines get better. They take jobs. They become more persuasive, more autonomous, and more deeply embedded in ordinary life.

People start depending on them. Economically, psychologically, politically. Eventually we discover that we have built systems whose capabilities can be increased faster than their behaviour can be controlled.

That story smuggles in a premise: capability sets the future.

It does not.

A nuclear weapon can destroy a city. Its physical power still tells us very little about when it will be used. Chemistry made the destruction of the ozone layer possible, but chemistry did not decide whether industries would keep producing the chemicals that caused it. Biological techniques can be weaponised without settling whether states will treat biological warfare as legitimate.

Between capability and consequence sits human judgement.

We decide what things are for. Then, sometimes clumsily and sometimes with surprising success, we build institutions around those decisions.

That may be the most hopeful fact about AI.

## Humans Make the Value Function

AI discussions often put human beings and machines on a single scoreboard. Intelligence. Productivity. Creativity. Usefulness.

On that scoreboard, the pessimistic argument is hard to dismiss. If a machine can do a task faster, cheaper, and eventually better, why keep paying a person to do it?

Because output is not the whole of value.

People go to live concerts even though recorded music costs less. They watch human beings play chess even though computers play better. They buy handmade objects when mass-produced versions are available. Parents usually regard raising their children as valuable even though childcare can be delegated.

None of this tells us exactly what future societies will value. It tells us where the real decision sits. Value is partly a social choice.

A society might decide that human agency, mastery, responsibility, participation, and flourishing matter in their own right. If it did, automated output would no longer be the only measure by which an AI economy was organised.

Children might be expected to learn mathematics before handing the work to a machine. Apprentices might learn a craft before supervising an AI that performs it. Some decisions might still require human judgement even when automation is cheaper.

People might retain meaningful roles in medicine, education, government, art, law, and care because taking part in those practices helps make a human life go well.

Those would be political choices. Politics exists because societies decide that some values are worth protecting from markets, technologies, and short-term incentives.

## The Missing AI Institution

That gives us a different way to see the AI governance problem. It is partly institutional.

Most current discussions imagine regulation as the answer: governments write rules, companies follow them, regulators impose penalties.

Powerful AI systems may require more than that.

Think about the legal system and interpersonal violence. Civilisation did not make violence disappear by teaching every citizen enough martial arts to defend themselves. It built institutions. Laws define prohibited conduct. Courts interpret the laws. Police investigate. Prosecutors bring cases. Judges decide them. Constitutions limit the people exercising those powers.

An individual does not need to overpower every criminal. Society concentrates defensive capacity in institutions designed for the job.

AI may eventually need an equivalent. Call it, provisionally, an AI constitutional order.

It would begin with principles defining the relationship between humans and artificial agents. When must a person retain meaningful control? What kinds of assistance are appropriate for children? When does an AI companion move from helpful interaction into exploitative attachment?

Then come the professional structures:

- inspectors and investigators
- specialist courts or tribunals
- licensing authorities
- technical enforcement units
- officials trained in machine behaviour, cybersecurity, behavioural manipulation, model evaluation, and human psychology

People given unusual powers would need duties and oversight. They might take oaths, as judges, police officers, physicians, soldiers, and other professionals already do.

Some of the enforcement would eventually be done by AI systems themselves. Not humanoid officers walking city streets. Software hunting software.

If malicious actors deploy autonomous malware, AI-enabled fraud, cyberweapons, or systems designed to manipulate other machines, human investigators may simply be too slow. Defensive institutions would need powerful AI of their own.

That creates an arms race between offence and defence. We already live with versions of it in cybersecurity, policing, intelligence, finance, and warfare.

Criminal law does not become pointless because criminals exist. Taxation does not become impossible because people evade it. Institutions rarely eliminate adversarial behaviour. They make order possible in its presence.

## But Who Guards the AI Guards?

Power creates its own problem.

An AI enforcement authority able to inspect models, restrict systems, access networks, or disable dangerous agents would possess extraordinary power. Governments could abuse it. Corporations could capture it. Officials could become corrupt. Political factions could bend it toward their interests.

This is not a special problem invented by artificial intelligence. It is the old problem of government.

Constitutional government exists because people have spent centuries trying to build institutions strong enough to govern without making them arbitrary.

The familiar machinery follows:

- separation of powers
- judicial review
- transparency requirements
- appeals
- independent oversight
- professional standards
- elections
- due process
- competing jurisdictions
- investigative journalism
- civil society

None of these mechanisms works perfectly. That is not their purpose. Their purpose is to make power contestable.

An AI constitutional order would need the same property. Decisions should be challengeable. Enforcement powers should require authorisation. Regulators should answer to other institutions. Rules should be revisable as the technology changes.

People often describe the alternatives as perfect control or inevitable catastrophe. History offers a less dramatic category: imperfect institutions that still change what is possible.

## Humanity Has Done This Before

There are good reasons to be cautious about institutional optimism. Collective action fails constantly. Governments delay. Firms lobby. States defect. Individuals pursue short-term gains at everyone else’s expense.

The tragedy of the commons is real.

It is not destiny.

When scientists established that chlorofluorocarbons were damaging the ozone layer, governments eventually created an international regime to reduce ozone-depleting substances. The Montreal Protocol became a global agreement. The UN Environment Programme says the ozone layer is now on a path toward recovery.

States have also tried to constrain technologies that create strong incentives to compete. The Nuclear Non-Proliferation Treaty aims to prevent the spread of nuclear weapons while supporting peaceful nuclear cooperation and disarmament. It has 191 parties. The Biological Weapons Convention prohibits the development, production, acquisition, transfer, stockpiling, and use of biological and toxin weapons, establishing an international legal norm against them.

These are not triumphal stories. Nuclear weapons still exist. Biological weapons remain a danger. Environmental agreements can weaken or fail.

Their importance is narrower. Human beings sometimes recognise that individually rational behaviour can produce collectively disastrous results. Sometimes they respond by inventing institutions that alter the incentives.

Institutions become easy to ignore when they work.

## The Real Human Superpower

Maybe we are using the wrong comparison when we ask whether AI will surpass humanity.

One human mind against one artificial system is a tidy contest. It is also a misleading one.

A person is forgetful, physically fragile, inconsistent, and short-lived. Human beings have another ability, though. We can build things that outlast and outscale us.

A university can accumulate knowledge for 800 years. A court can make decisions using precedents created by people who died centuries ago. A scientific discipline can coordinate the work of millions who never meet. A state can collect taxes from millions of citizens and maintain infrastructure none of them could build alone.

Institutions are the strange technology that lets vulnerable biological creatures create forms of agency larger than any individual life.

They also evolve.

So the arrival of extremely capable AI does not mean that existing institutions must stretch until they break. We can build new ones.

That is a possibility, not a forecast.

The optimistic case for humanity is not that people will always make wise decisions. History gives us no reason to believe that. It is that people can recognise a new category of problem and invent social machinery for it.

Police forces did not always exist. Constitutional courts did not always exist. Central banks did not always exist. International arms-control regimes did not always exist.

They were inventions.

AI governance can be one too.

## The Constitution After Intelligence

Assume machines become more intelligent than individual people.

The decisive question will be what humanity builds around them.

A civilisation surrounded by extraordinary machine intelligence could still decide that human development matters. It could protect childhood from premature automation. It could preserve parts of education and work where people need to acquire competence rather than merely receive an outcome.

It could regulate artificial companionship when dependency becomes exploitative. It could stop companies from treating maximal engagement as the only measure of a successful relationship between a person and a machine.

And when AI systems become dangerous, that civilisation could use institutional power against them: investigators assisted by machines, courts advised by technical experts, defensive AIs looking for hostile AIs, constitutional rules governing all of them.

There would be failures. Capture attempts. Corruption. Evasion. Political fights. Loopholes. Scandals. Reform.

In other words, politics.

That may sound mundane beside the usual visions of superintelligence, extinction, abundance, or transcendence.

But civilisation is mostly made of mundane machinery.

Rules. Offices. Procedures. Duties. Checks. Appeals.

The future of intelligence may depend on something intelligence cannot decide by itself: what humans choose to preserve, and what they are willing to build in order to preserve it.
