# The Most Important AI Technology Might Be an Institution

**Fabrizio Costa**  | September 2026

The standard story about artificial intelligence goes something like this: The machines get better. They take more jobs. They become more persuasive, more autonomous, more embedded in daily life. Humans become dependent on them, perhaps economically, perhaps psychologically, perhaps politically. At some point, we discover that we have built systems whose capabilities are easier to increase than to control.

This story contains a hidden assumption: that technological capability determines the future.

But human societies have never worked quite that way.

A nuclear weapon can destroy a city, yet its physical capability does not tell us when it will be used. A chemical can destroy stratospheric ozone, yet chemistry alone does not determine whether industries continue producing it. Biological techniques can be weaponized, yet their existence does not settle whether states will regard biological warfare as legitimate.

Between capability and consequence sits something stranger: human judgment.

We decide what things are worth. Then, sometimes clumsily and sometimes remarkably effectively, we build institutions around those judgments.

That may be the most important source of optimism about AI.

## Humans Make the Value Function

Artificial intelligence is usually discussed as though human beings and machines were competing along a single axis: intelligence, productivity, creativity, perhaps even usefulness.

On that axis, the pessimistic argument looks powerful. If an AI system can perform a task faster, more cheaply, and eventually better than a human being, why would anyone continue paying a human to do it?

But economics is never merely a measurement of physical capability. Value depends on what people decide to value.

We already pay enormous amounts of money for things precisely because humans did them. People attend live concerts when recorded music is cheaper. They watch humans play chess even though computers play better. They buy handmade objects despite the availability of mass production. Parents generally regard raising their own children as valuable even though childcare can be delegated.

These examples do not establish what future societies will value. They illustrate the prior question: what counts as valuable is partly a social choice.

Suppose societies eventually decide that human agency, mastery, responsibility, participation, and flourishing are goods in themselves. Then maximizing automated output would cease to be the sole criterion by which an AI economy was organized.

A society could decide that children should learn mathematics before routinely delegating it to machines. That apprentices should acquire a craft before supervising AI systems that perform it. That certain decisions should require human judgment even when automation is cheaper. That humans should retain meaningful roles in medicine, education, government, art, law, and care because participation in those practices contributes to human development.

These would be political choices.

And politics exists precisely because societies routinely decide that some outcomes matter enough to constrain markets, technologies, and individual incentives.

## The Missing AI Institution

This suggests that the great unresolved AI problem may be institutional rather than purely technical.

Today, discussions of AI governance often revolve around regulation: governments write rules, companies comply with them, regulators impose penalties.

But sufficiently powerful AI systems may require something more elaborate.

Imagine an institution whose relationship to artificial intelligence resembles the relationship between the legal system and interpersonal violence.

Civilization did not eliminate violence by teaching every citizen enough martial arts to defend themselves. It created institutions. Laws specify prohibited conduct. Courts interpret those laws. Police investigate violations. Prosecutors bring cases. Judges adjudicate them. Constitutions constrain the people exercising those powers.

Individuals therefore do not need to personally overpower every criminal actor. Society concentrates defensive capacity in institutions designed for that purpose.

AI may eventually need an equivalent.

Call it, provisionally, an AI constitutional order.

It would begin with principles defining the relationship between humans and artificial agents. Some of those principles might concern autonomy: when must a human retain meaningful control? Others might concern development: what forms of AI assistance are appropriate for children? Others would concern dependency: when does an AI companion cross from helpful interaction into exploitative psychological attachment?

Then come professional structures:

- inspectors and investigators
- courts or specialist tribunals
- licensing authorities
- technical enforcement units
- officials trained specifically in machine behavior, cybersecurity, behavioral manipulation, model evaluation, and human psychology

People entrusted with unusual powers might take oaths and operate under statutory duties, just as judges, police officers, physicians, soldiers, and other professionals already accept obligations attached to their roles.

And eventually there would probably be AI police.

Not humanoid officers patrolling streets. Software systems hunting software systems.

If malicious actors deploy autonomous malware, AI enabled fraud, cyberweapons, or systems designed to manipulate other machines, human investigators operating at human speed may be hopelessly outmatched. Enforcement institutions would therefore use powerful AI systems themselves.

The result would look familiar in one respect. There would be an arms race between offense and defense.

We already live with that condition in cybersecurity, policing, intelligence, finance, and warfare. The existence of criminals does not make criminal law pointless. The existence of tax evasion does not make taxation impossible. Institutional systems rarely eliminate adversarial behavior. They make sustained social order possible despite it.

## But Who Guards the AI Guards?

Of course, creating powerful institutions introduces an ancient problem.

Who controls them?

An AI enforcement authority capable of inspecting models, restricting systems, accessing networks, or disabling dangerous agents would possess extraordinary power. Governments could abuse it. Corporations could capture it. Officials could become corrupt. Political factions could bend it toward their interests.

There is no magical solution here because this is not a uniquely artificial intelligence problem.

Constitutional government exists because humanity has spent centuries wrestling with precisely this dilemma: how to create institutions powerful enough to govern while preventing those institutions from becoming arbitrary.

The familiar machinery follows from that problem:

- separation of powers
- judicial review
- transparency requirements
- appeals
- independent oversight
- professional standards
- elections
- due process
- competing jurisdictions
- investigative journalism
- civil society

None of these mechanisms works perfectly.

Their purpose is not perfection. Their purpose is to make power contestable.

An AI constitutional order would need the same property. Decisions would have to be challengeable. Enforcement powers would require authorization. Regulators would themselves require regulators. Rules would need revision as technology changed.

The alternative is sometimes framed as a choice between perfect control and inevitable catastrophe. History offers a messier category: imperfect institutions that nevertheless change outcomes dramatically.

## Humanity Has Done This Before

There are reasons to treat institutional optimism cautiously. Collective action fails constantly. Governments procrastinate. Firms lobby. States defect. Individuals pursue short term gains at everyone else’s expense.

The tragedy of the commons is real.

But it is not destiny.

When scientists established that chlorofluorocarbons were damaging Earth’s ozone layer, governments eventually constructed an international regime for reducing ozone depleting substances. The Montreal Protocol became a global agreement, and the UN Environment Programme reports that the ozone layer is now on a path toward recovery. 

States have also created international regimes intended to constrain technologies whose destructive potential creates powerful incentives for competition. The Nuclear Non-Proliferation Treaty seeks to prevent the spread of nuclear weapons while advancing peaceful nuclear cooperation and disarmament; 191 states are parties to it.  The Biological Weapons Convention prohibits the development, production, acquisition, transfer, stockpiling, and use of biological and toxin weapons and has established an international legal norm against them. 

None of these examples amounts to a triumphal story about human rationality. Nuclear weapons still exist. Biological weapons remain a danger. Environmental agreements can weaken or fail.

Their significance is narrower and more interesting.

Humans sometimes recognize that individually rational behavior can create collectively disastrous outcomes. And sometimes they respond by inventing institutions that change the incentive structure itself.

That capacity is easy to overlook because institutions become invisible when they work.

## The Real Human Superpower

This points toward a different meaning of human potential.

When people worry about AI surpassing humanity, they often compare one human mind with one artificial system.

That may be the wrong unit of comparison.

A human being is cognitively limited, physically vulnerable, forgetful, inconsistent, and short lived.

Yet humans possess an unusual ability to construct entities that are none of those things.

A university can accumulate knowledge for 800 years. A court can make decisions according to precedents established by people who died centuries ago. A scientific discipline can coordinate the intellectual labor of millions of people who never meet. A state can collect taxes from tens of millions of citizens and use them to maintain infrastructure none of those citizens could build individually.

Institutions allow fragile biological creatures to create forms of collective agency that exceed the capabilities and lifespans of their individual members.

And institutions themselves evolve.

This is why the arrival of extremely capable AI does not necessarily imply that today’s institutions must somehow stretch far enough to contain it.

We can build new ones.

That proposition is neither a prediction nor a guarantee.

It is a statement about potentiality.

The optimistic case for humanity is not that human beings will always make wise decisions. History gives no basis for such confidence. It is that human beings can recognize new categories of problem and construct social machinery that did not previously exist.

Police forces did not always exist. Constitutional courts did not always exist. Central banks did not always exist. International arms control regimes did not always exist.

They were inventions.

AI governance can be an invention too.

## The Constitution After Intelligence

Perhaps, then, the decisive question of the AI century will not be whether machines become more intelligent than individual people.

Assume they do.

The deeper question is what humanity builds around them.

A civilization surrounded by machines of extraordinary intelligence could still decide that human development matters. It could protect childhood from premature automation. Preserve domains in which people need to acquire competence rather than merely obtain outcomes. Regulate artificial companionship where dependency becomes exploitative. Prevent companies from treating maximal engagement as the only measure of a successful human AI relationship.

And when AI systems themselves become dangerous, that civilization could deploy institutional power against them: investigators assisted by machines, courts informed by technical experts, defensive AIs identifying hostile AIs, constitutional rules determining what all of them are allowed to do.

There would be failures. There would be capture attempts. There would be corruption, evasion, technological races, political battles, loopholes, scandals, and reform.

In other words, there would be politics.

That may sound disappointingly mundane compared with visions of superintelligence, extinction, abundance, or technological transcendence.

But civilization is largely made of mundane machinery.

Rules. Offices. Procedures. Duties. Checks. Appeals. Institutions.

The future of intelligence may ultimately depend on something intelligence alone cannot determine: what humans decide is worth preserving, and what they are willing to build to preserve it.
