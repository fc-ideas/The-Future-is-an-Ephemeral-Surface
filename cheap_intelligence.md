# When Intelligence Is Cheap, Meaning Is Rare

AI makes competent thinking abundant. That does not make it meaningful. Someone still has to decide where to look, what to ask, and what deserves to ship. The future is not humans vs. machines; it’s people who can steer a flood of intelligence vs. people who get swept away. When the cost of thought collapses, the premium shifts to people who can choose what matters and ignore what doesn’t.

Below are three forces—and how to handle them—with the lens of a pragmatic operator. Each builds on the last: first you decide what to pay attention to, then you direct how systems work, and finally you judge what should survive.

---

## 1) Expertise Shifts From Cognition to Curation

Intelligence is everywhere; judgment isn’t. Two founders tackle the same market report. One feeds the entire dataset to an AI and accepts the first slick answer. The other slices the data, asks for three opposing theses, and checks each against ground truth. The second founder wins—not by thinking faster, but by choosing where to shine the light. The gap is not talent but taste: knowing which questions deserve attention and which answers deserve suspicion.

Try this:
- Decide the question: “Which assumption, if wrong, kills this?” beats “Summarize this PDF.” The sharper the question, the less noise the model can hide behind.
- Set constraints: Specify sources, formats, and stop rules to leave less room for confident nonsense. Boundaries turn a generic response into something checkable.
- Judge returns: Keep a short list of facts to verify; compare outputs to reality, not style. Verification keeps you from mistaking fluency for truth.

Pitfall: cheap intelligence turns loose prompts into polished errors. Curation is the guardrail. Without deliberate framing, you get faster wrong answers that feel right.

---

## 2) You Orchestrate Systems, Not Hold a Brush

Once you curate, you have to direct. Modern models feel like a team on call, but they are pattern-matchers, not real experts. Think director, not painter—script the roles and keep score. The work shifts from doing the strokes yourself to arranging who speaks when, with what constraints, and under which referee.

Try this:
- Cast roles deliberately: “Act as a skeptical regulator” produces different checks than “brainstorm features.” Role prompts swap out lenses without rebuilding the whole system.
- Chain perspectives: Ask for a proposal, then a red-team pass, then a costed revision. Sequence matters. Ordering forces the model to encounter friction before it settles.
- Keep a referee: Use retrieval and external data to anchor outputs; never let the ensemble grade itself. External anchors stop circular self-consistency from masquerading as truth.

Failure mode: orchestration without facts creates a chorus of agreeing hallucinations. Keep one foot in reality. A director with no script supervision just gets louder fiction.

---

## 3) Creation Is Easy; Discrimination Is the Scarce Skill

After directing, you still need to approve. Machines can draft endlessly, but their sense of novelty, taste, or consequence is thin. Synthetic evals exist, yet they inherit the model’s blind spots. Humans still choose what is coherent, useful, and defensible. The scarce move is not pressing “generate” but knowing which outputs stand up to reality, audience, and risk.

Try this:
- Define “good” before generating (accuracy, originality, tone, compliance). Clear criteria make review objective instead of vibe-based.
- Use structured checks: rubrics, test cases, sample counterexamples. Repeatable checks expose weaknesses that surface-level reading misses.
- Bring context: audience, risk, downstream impact—things the model does not know. Judgment lives where stakes and people meet, not in the prompt window.

Remember: in a world of generators, the rare asset is a sharp discriminator with context and taste. The approval step is where you turn infinite drafts into one decision you’re willing to own.

---

# Toward a Useful Loop

Cheap intelligence is not the point; directed intelligence is. The skill to practice is standing in the deluge and saying, “This way”—with reasons. Run one loop this week: pick a decision, pose a precise question, script roles with constraints, test against a fact, and revise. Thinking above the machine, not instead of it, is the job. The loop is small on purpose: repetition makes the transitions—curate, orchestrate, discriminate—feel natural instead of forced.
