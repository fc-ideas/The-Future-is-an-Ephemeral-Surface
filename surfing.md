# Learning to Surf the Future

**Fabrizio Costa**  | March 2026

For most of human history, the central skill of adulthood was stability. You learned a trade, you occupied a social role, and—barring war or famine—the world you died in resembled the world you were born into. Culture moved, but at geological speed. The practical competence of a person was therefore depth: knowing one craft thoroughly, mastering one institutional environment, inhabiting one set of norms.

Today that competence is quietly obsolete.

The defining feature of modern life is not merely technological change; it is *rate of change as a lived experience*. The difference matters. Previous generations experienced innovation as events. We experience innovation as background conditions. New devices, new interfaces, new conventions of communication, new etiquette around identity and work—these no longer arrive as disruptions separated by decades. They arrive continuously, overlapping, before the previous adjustment is complete.

The right metaphor is no longer construction. It is surfing.

Standing on land rewards solidity. The ground does not renegotiate its rules. Once you understand gravity and friction, the problem is solved. Social life used to resemble this: institutions were slow, professions hereditary in practice if not in law, and knowledge aged slowly enough that expertise accumulated without frequent resets. A person could orient their life toward permanence.

A wave behaves differently. A wave has structure, but no stability. You cannot master a wave once and for all. You can only maintain balance moment by moment.

This increasingly describes ordinary life. Consider something trivial: using a personal device. Twenty years ago, learning an operating system was a one-time investment. Today interfaces mutate constantly—gestures replace buttons, voice replaces typing, automation replaces menus, AI replaces search. The user is no longer expected to memorize procedures. The user is expected to adapt.

The competence being selected for is not knowledge but equilibrium.

The individual now operates in a permanently moving environment: software updates, shifting workplace tools, changing expectations about communication speed, algorithmic mediation of attention, and the integration of machine intelligence into routine tasks. The result is a psychological condition new at scale: the average person lives inside a system whose rules are updated faster than they can be fully learned.

On land, falling is unusual. In the ocean, falling is normal. The relevant question is recovery time.

This reframes education, expertise, and even identity. The older model assumed a stable self placed into a stable world. The new model assumes a stable *capacity to rebalance* within an unstable world. A useful worker is not the one who knows a specific software package or methodology; it is the one who can abandon it without distress and adopt another within days.

Artificial intelligence accelerates this shift because it removes the last refuge of permanence: cognitive routine. Previously, when tools changed, reasoning remained human territory. Now the reasoning substrate itself is shared with machines. Writing, coding, planning, research—activities that anchored professional identity—are partially externalized. The individual must repeatedly renegotiate what their contribution actually is.

The surfboard, in this metaphor, is understanding rather than control. You cannot stop the wave. Attempting to resist it leads to exhaustion and irrelevance. You can only read its direction early, position yourself appropriately, and maintain balance as it breaks.

This does not mean shallow living. It means a different kind of depth: not attachment to specific tools, but fluency in transitions. Not expertise in one interface, but comfort with unfamiliar interfaces. Not certainty about norms, but tolerance for ambiguity.

We are entering a society where adulthood is less about establishing a fixed position and more about maintaining dynamic stability. Careers will resemble sequences of temporary equilibria. Skills will decay faster than reputations. The valuable person will be the one who falls, stands up, and reorients quickly.

The future individual is therefore not a builder standing on solid ground. It is a surfer: alert, adaptive, continuously learning the shape of forces they cannot halt but can navigate.

The wave is not coming.

It is already under us.
