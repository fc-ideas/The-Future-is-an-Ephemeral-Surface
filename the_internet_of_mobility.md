# The Internet of Mobility

**Fabrizio Costa**  | April 2026

There is a familiar pattern in how complex systems evolve. At first, everything is bespoke: tightly coupled, inefficient, fragile. Then a unifying abstraction appears. For the internet, it was the packet: a small, standardized unit that could move through any network, independent of the hardware carrying it.

The internet did not standardize every cable, computer, or data center. It standardized the unit of movement.

Transport never had its packet moment.

Today mobility is still organized around vehicles. Cars, buses, trains, planes: each is a silo with its own infrastructure, schedule, safety model, and user experience. The weak points are the boundaries between them. The taxi to the station. The platform change. The security queue. The gate. The last mile. Every transition adds friction, latency, and cognitive load.

What if transport behaved less like a collection of vehicles and more like a network?

## The Pod as Packet

The idea begins with a unit: a personal mobility pod.

Not a car, not a seat, not quite a room. A sealed, self-contained environment sized for one person, a family unit, or a small group. It has minimal independent mobility: enough battery and drive to reposition itself, dock, undock, enter a home bay, cross a short forecourt, or align with a carrier.

Think tens of meters, not miles.

Its real purpose is to be carried.

Inside, the pod is yours. Workspace, lighting, screens, entertainment, storage, communications, climate. You do not board transport in the usual sense. You bring your environment with you. The pod becomes identity, interface, payload, and protected personal volume.

The important move is not the pod itself. It is what becomes possible when pods are composable.

## Assembly

Instead of moving people between vehicles, the system moves pods between carriers.

A small autonomous carrier collects pods from homes or local docking points. These carriers aggregate into larger frames: bus-scale, train-scale, ship-scale, aircraft-compatible, or whatever other modes the network supports. At each layer, the system optimizes for energy, capacity, speed, and timing.

You do not transfer. Your pod is routed.

At one moment it is part of a local carrier. Then it is docked into a larger flow, aligned with dozens or hundreds of others. Later it separates again and moves through the final stretch. From the outside, the system is constantly coupling and decoupling. From the inside, the journey is continuous.

This is the key reversal. Existing mass transport asks humans to adapt to the efficient unit: walk to the station, wait for the train, find the platform, board, disembark, transfer. A modular system lets the infrastructure adapt around the passenger unit.

The routing problem becomes computational rather than experiential.

## Efficiency by Aggregation

Transport efficiency scales with grouping. A single passenger in a private car is energetically expensive. A full train is not. The trouble is that today we usually obtain efficiency by imposing shared schedules and shared spaces on people.

Pods separate the two.

They can travel alone when necessary, but the network continuously searches for opportunities to assemble them into larger formations. It solves a packing problem across space and time: how to cluster pods into carriers while respecting destination, urgency, energy use, network load, passenger preferences, and safety constraints.

This is close to what the internet does with data packets, except the packets weigh hundreds of kilograms and carry humans.

That difference matters. It makes the analogy imperfect. But it also clarifies the ambition: define a standard unit of mobility, then let roads, stations, carriers, schedules, and cities reorganize around it.

## The Interior World

Once the pod becomes the stable unit, the passenger experience can be designed independently of the vehicle carrying it.

Screens are not just entertainment surfaces. They become adaptive interfaces between the body and motion. They can anticipate acceleration, turns, braking, and vibration, rendering the outside world in ways that make inertia legible. A bend in the track can be softened visually before the body feels it. A change in speed can be represented as part of the environment rather than as a surprise.

Motion sickness is partly a conflict between what the body feels and what the eyes see. A pod that knows its trajectory can reduce that conflict.

But once the view is mediated, the constraint disappears.

The pod can show the real world, reconstructed from cameras and predictive models. Or it can edit it: remove congestion, add light, clarify landmarks, calm visual noise. Or it can replace it entirely. Tuscan hills. A night drive through Tokyo. A slow glide under an imagined ocean. A route across a Martian plain.

Movement becomes not only displacement, but experience design.

## Safety as a Primitive

If the pod is the unit, safety can also be designed at the level of the individual.

Today many transport systems protect a shared cabin full of loosely constrained people. A pod-based system protects a single enclosed environment. The pod can become a cocoon: layered structure, adaptive materials, distributed airbags, fire-resistant shells, independent air, internal restraint, emergency communication, and fail-safe docking.

In a critical event, the interior responds locally. It cushions, isolates, locks down, signals, and preserves life support. The carrier may fail, but the protected unit remains legible to the network.

This changes the geometry of risk. Safety is no longer averaged across a crowd or a cabin. It is guaranteed, or at least certified, per unit.

That certification would be hard. But the abstraction is powerful: once the pod is the safety envelope, every carrier does not need to reinvent the passenger cabin. It needs to safely carry the standard.

## Private Space, Public Network

The persistent tension in transport is between autonomy and efficiency.

Cars offer privacy and control, but waste energy, space, and attention. Public transport is efficient, but it asks people to share schedules, proximity, noise, uncertainty, and social exposure. Many passengers do not object to public transport because they reject collectivity in principle. They object because the shared layer is imposed at the level of the body.

Pods decouple privacy from network sharing.

You remain in your own environment, but that environment plugs into a public system. At scale, this hybrid could outperform both private cars and traditional public transport: high utilization without the psychological overhead of mass proximity.

It does not eliminate social life. It relocates it.

## The Social Layer

On longer journeys, larger carriers can include shared corridors, lounges, quiet rooms, cafes, meeting spaces, or family areas. Pods open onto them when passengers choose to participate. Social space becomes optional and explicit, rather than a side effect of being placed next to a stranger.

That choice could become part of routing itself.

You might optimize for speed. You might optimize for comfort. You might also optimize for connection. If you opt in, the system can align trajectories so that compatible travelers share time and space: overlapping interests, complementary work, common destinations, similar constraints, mutual curiosity.

This would need care. Asymmetric matching would be invasive. Manipulative social routing would be dystopian. But a mutual, transparent, opt-in layer could make mobility a medium for useful serendipity.

Sometimes nothing would happen. Sometimes a conversation would fill an hour. Occasionally something would persist beyond the journey.

## A Journey

Consider a family leaving the UK to visit relatives in the Italian countryside.

They step into their pods at home. Luggage is stored below, sealed within the structure, tracked and inaccessible until arrival. The pods close, not to isolate them, but to define their space.

A local carrier arrives. The pods align, lock in, and begin moving. Inside, the family remains together. Screens synchronize. Games pass between children. Conversations continue. Parents can see what younger children are doing. A child can share a larger pod with an adult when needed.

They do not think about transfers.

At the edge of the city, the local carrier joins a larger flow. The pods are integrated into a long-haul system. Time stretches. The outside world shifts: perhaps the actual landscape, perhaps a reconstructed view, perhaps something softer and more imaginative.

Later, the system contracts again. The pods detach into smaller formations. A train-like phase gives way to a regional carrier, then a local route, then the last approach through narrow roads.

At the end, they arrive at a house in the countryside.

The same system could bring elderly relatives in the other direction: gently, continuously, without the discontinuities that make long journeys difficult for people with limited mobility.

From the inside, it feels like one movement. From the outside, it is a sequence of transformations.

## Infrastructure as Router

If pods are packets, infrastructure becomes a router.

Stations are no longer primarily places where people move. They become switching nodes where pods are sorted, buffered, charged, inspected, cleaned, transferred, and recombined. Airports become high-throughput pod handling systems. Roads near urban centers become feeders into aggregation hubs. Parking becomes less important than docking.

Urban design changes accordingly: fewer idle vehicles, fewer waiting rooms, fewer exposed transfer points; more flow-through architecture, charging surfaces, maintenance bays, and local pickup cells.

Control also shifts. Timetables become less central. Routing becomes continuous, algorithmic, and demand-responsive. The system starts to resemble a distributed computation: a city-scale machine for matching pods, carriers, corridors, energy, and time.

## The Standard Problem

For any of this to work, the pod must become a standard.

The internet scales because packets are format-agnostic. Any network that understands the protocol can carry them. A modular transport system would need the same kind of interoperability across manufacturers, cities, and modes.

Dimensions, docking mechanisms, power interfaces, data protocols, crash structures, cleaning systems, emergency access, fire rules, cybersecurity requirements, and passenger rights would all need to converge. Without that convergence, the system fragments into incompatible ecosystems and the packet abstraction collapses.

This may be the hardest part.

Transport has often resisted standardization. Rail gauges diverged. Charging systems competed. Airline cabins are not modular across fleets. Cities procure infrastructure on political and budgetary cycles, not protocol cycles.

The limiting factor may be less engineering than coordination.

## Friction Points

The idea is clean at the abstraction level. Reality is less forgiving.

Weight is nontrivial. A pod adds mass around every passenger, and moving hundreds of kilograms per person only makes sense if aggregation produces large enough efficiency gains. The system would need exceptional utilization, lightweight materials, and careful lifecycle accounting.

Safety certification would be brutal. A pod that moves through road, rail, sea, and air systems couples regulatory domains that barely speak the same language today. Failure modes multiply: a bad dock, a missed connection, a carrier fault, a routing error, a cyberattack, a passenger emergency inside a sealed unit.

Trust is another constraint. People tolerate inconvenience when it gives them control. Giving up the steering wheel, the platform choice, the visible route, or the right to improvise requires confidence in the entire stack.

Cost may be decisive. A pod is not disposable. It is closer to a durable asset: part vehicle, part room, part device. Ownership models would shape adoption as much as technology would. Private ownership, leasing, municipal fleets, workplace subscriptions, hotel-like rentals, medical pods, child-safe pods, luxury pods: each model creates different incentives and inequities.

There is also the political question. A network that routes bodies is a network that can prioritize, exclude, surveil, delay, and price movement. Protocols are not neutral simply because they are efficient.

## Transport After the Vehicle

What makes the idea compelling is not that it predicts the future. Predictions are cheap. The value is that it reframes transport at the right level of abstraction.

Instead of optimizing vehicles, it defines a unit of mobility and lets everything else reorganize around it. Instead of asking whether cars become autonomous, trains become faster, or aircraft become cleaner, it asks what happens when the passenger environment becomes continuous and the transport layer becomes modular.

The result would not simply be a better taxi, train, or plane. It would be a shared protocol for moving private human spaces through public infrastructure.

Science fiction has always done this at its best. It does not provide a product roadmap. It names a desirable interface to the world.

A modular transport system built around personal pods would do the same.

Not a forecast.

A target.

The pod is not the point.

The protocol is.
