# The Social Contract for Artificial Intelligence

**Fabrizio Costa**  | March 2026

Silicon Valley has a simple incentive structure: build technology, capture markets, scale fast. For decades this model has produced extraordinary innovation. It has also produced something else—technologies that reshape society faster than society can adapt.

Artificial intelligence is about to amplify that dynamic.

Unlike previous waves of automation, AI systems increasingly make decisions, generate knowledge, and perform cognitive work. Economists describe technologies of this kind as **general-purpose technologies**—the rare class of inventions that transform entire economies, like electricity or computing. These technologies tend to unlock massive productivity gains while simultaneously reorganizing labor markets and institutions.

Thomas Bresnahan and Manuel Trajtenberg first formalized this concept in 1995, describing general-purpose technologies as innovations that spread across industries and trigger cascading waves of complementary innovation.
Source: Bresnahan & Trajtenberg, *General Purpose Technologies: Engines of Growth?*, Journal of Econometrics (1995).

AI clearly fits the pattern. It is becoming infrastructure.

But the current governance model for AI is essentially accidental. Market incentives alone determine which systems get built and which ones scale. Governments mostly react after the fact.

The question is no longer whether AI will reshape society. The question is whether we can design incentives that steer its development toward outcomes we actually want.

What might that look like?

---

## Measuring What Matters

One of the problems with current AI governance is that we rarely measure the right things.

Companies track revenue, user growth, and compute efficiency. Regulators track safety and compliance. But the societal impact of AI systems is harder to quantify.

Imagine instead a set of metrics that evaluate AI systems along multiple dimensions:

• job creation and workforce transformation
• economic productivity gains
• transparency and accountability
• the degree to which systems expand—or erode—human decision-making power

That last one may be the most important.

Many AI systems today aim to replace human decisions entirely. Recommendation engines decide what we watch. Navigation systems decide where we drive. Algorithmic systems increasingly determine hiring, credit scoring, and insurance risk.

Human-centered AI researchers have long argued that AI should **augment human intelligence rather than replace it**.
Source: Ben Shneiderman, *Human-Centered Artificial Intelligence*, Journal of the American Medical Informatics Association (2020).

But augmentation does not happen automatically. It depends on design choices—and on incentives.

One proposal is to measure what we might call **human agency**.

How much control does a user retain?
How transparent is the reasoning process?
Does the system help users learn and improve their own decisions?

There is no standardized “agency index” yet. But the idea echoes existing international frameworks. The OECD’s AI principles explicitly emphasize that AI should strengthen human autonomy and decision-making.
Source: OECD, *Principles on Artificial Intelligence* (2019).

If we can measure agency, we can reward it.

## Incentivizing the Right AI

Now imagine linking those measurements to economic incentives.

Governments already shape innovation through tax credits, research funding, and procurement programs. Public investment has historically played a central role in technologies ranging from semiconductors to the internet.

Economist Mariana Mazzucato documents how many foundational technologies emerged from public funding rather than purely private markets.
Source: Mazzucato, *The Entrepreneurial State* (2013).

AI could follow a similar model.

Suppose a portion of tax revenue were directed into a **public AI development fund**. Companies and research institutes could apply for funding, but the evaluation criteria would go beyond technical performance.

Systems would be assessed on societal impact metrics:

• workforce outcomes
• augmentation of human capability
• transparency and accountability
• long-term economic benefits

The more an AI system improves these outcomes, the stronger the funding support.

In effect, innovation would still compete—but along axes that reflect societal goals rather than just market dominance.

---

## Avoiding the Winner-Take-All Problem

There is another structural issue in the AI economy: concentration.

Training large models requires enormous computational resources. This naturally favors a small number of extremely large companies.

If public funding simply reinforces the same players, the result would be predictable: an even more centralized AI ecosystem.

A different funding architecture could help.

Instead of awarding a single massive grant, funds could be distributed in **tiers**.

For example:

• a small number of top performers receive large grants
• a larger group receives medium grants
• an even larger pool receives smaller innovation grants

This kind of structure ensures that excellence is rewarded while still supporting a broad innovation ecosystem. It is similar in spirit to the diversified funding approaches used by many research councils.

The key point is simple: avoid designing systems where the biggest company automatically gets everything.

## A New Model of AI Governance

But who decides which systems deserve support?

Traditional regulation tends to rely on expert panels. That makes sense for highly technical questions, but it also creates a legitimacy problem. Citizens are affected by these technologies but rarely participate in shaping them.

An alternative approach blends expertise with democratic participation.

Experts—nominated by institutions such as universities, industry bodies, and regulatory agencies—would evaluate the technical dimensions of AI systems.

At the same time, a **citizens’ panel** selected by stratified random sampling would evaluate real-world impact.

This idea builds on a growing movement toward deliberative democratic institutions. Citizens’ assemblies have already been used in several countries to address complex policy issues, including climate policy and constitutional reform.
Source: OECD, *Innovative Citizen Participation and New Democratic Institutions* (2020).

Participants are selected to represent the demographic diversity of society and are given time and information to deliberate before making decisions.

In an AI context, such panels could evaluate questions like:

Does this system improve people's ability to make decisions?
Does it reduce opportunities or expand them?
Does it create new forms of dependency?

Experts and citizens would score policies independently, and the final outcome would combine both perspectives.

Technical competence meets lived experience.

---

## The Missing Social Contract

Every transformative technology eventually forces society to renegotiate its social contract.

Electricity reshaped cities and labor.
Automobiles transformed infrastructure and regulation.
The internet forced us to rethink information and privacy.

AI is pushing us toward a similar moment.

The question is not simply how powerful AI systems become. It is what kinds of incentives guide their development.

Do we reward systems that replace people?

Or systems that amplify what people can do?

Markets alone cannot answer that question. Markets optimize for profit signals, not for long-term social outcomes.

But with carefully designed incentives—measuring the right things, distributing resources wisely, and involving citizens in governance—we could align technological progress with human flourishing.

In other words, we could build a social contract for artificial intelligence.

The future of AI might depend less on algorithms than on the institutions we build around them.
