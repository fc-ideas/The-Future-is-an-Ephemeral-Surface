# The Ranking Machine

**Fabrizio Costa**  | June 2026

Humans do not simply want things. They want reasons for wanting them.

That may be our strangest feature. A fox wants food, shelter, sex, and safety. A human wants those too, but almost immediately wraps them in a story. The hunt becomes honour. The shelter becomes property. Sex becomes morality. Safety becomes nation. The future becomes destiny.

The animal predicts. The human justifies the prediction.

This is where civilisation begins: not with tools, not with fire, not even with language, but with the unbearable pressure of planning under uncertainty. A mind that can imagine tomorrow has to decide what tomorrow is for. It cannot optimise without a target. It cannot sacrifice without an explanation. It cannot ask others to sacrifice without a theory.

So every human society builds an abstract framework under its practical goals. It is never enough to say: we need food, land, children, labour, security. The tribe needs ancestors. The kingdom needs divine order. The republic needs rights. The party needs history. The market needs freedom. The algorithm needs intelligence.

Underneath all of them is the same architecture: a predictive machine trying to turn chaos into rank.

The world is too large to understand directly, so humans flatten it. We convert a dense, multidimensional reality into a single contextual scale. Pure and impure. Saved and damned. Noble and common. Civilised and barbarian. Revolutionary and reactionary. Productive and unproductive. Efficient and inefficient. Smart and obsolete.

The scale changes. The machinery does not.

A society can tolerate many differences, but it struggles to tolerate many final measures of value. Somewhere, quietly or violently, a dominant ranking appears. It tells people who is above and who is below, which actions count and which do not, which sacrifices are noble and which are waste. It gives hierarchy a moral grammar.

Religion did this with the vertical axis of the sacred. The highest thing was not merely strong but holy. Kings borrowed light from heaven. Priests administered the invisible scale. The peasant’s suffering could be made meaningful because the real accounting happened elsewhere, outside ordinary life, in a cosmic ledger beyond appeal.

Then came the Enlightenment and attempted a jailbreak. It did not abolish the ranking machine. It changed the unit. Reason replaced revelation. Progress replaced salvation. The future moved from heaven into history. Humanity would no longer climb toward God, but toward knowledge, liberty, and mastery of nature.

It was a magnificent mutation. It was also still a hierarchy.

Now the old question returned in secular clothes: who is most rational, most advanced, most fit to govern, most aligned with the direction of history? Once progress becomes the sacred axis, backwardness becomes sin. The missionary can become the reformer. The priest can become the expert. The empire can become the development programme.

Communism and capitalism look like enemies, and in many ways they are. But from this angle they are rival ranking systems.

Communism tries to collapse inherited hierarchy by ranking people through class position and historical role. The worker becomes the moral centre because the worker represents the future. The capitalist becomes not only an economic actor but a residue of an earlier stage. The final promise is a classless society, but the path to it runs through a ferocious temporary scale: revolutionary consciousness above false consciousness, party discipline above individual desire, history above the person.

Capitalism uses a colder metric. It ranks through price, productivity, ownership, credit, and market success. It has no need to declare a cosmic purpose, because the ranking is embedded in transactions. The market appears neutral because no single priest announces the verdict. But the verdict arrives anyway, every day, as salary, rent, debt, attention, access, status.

In both cases, the human animal is still trying to solve the same predictive problem. What should we do next? Who should coordinate it? Who should receive more resources? Who is allowed to command? Who can be ignored?

Politics is what happens when a society fights over the ranking function.

The terrifying thing about artificial intelligence is not only that it may become powerful. It is that it arrives as a new candidate for the top of the scale.

For centuries, human beings ranked themselves by proximity to gods, reason, race, class, capital, nation, or expertise. Now a new axis is forming: intelligence itself, detached from flesh. Not wisdom. Not goodness. Not beauty. Not courage. Intelligence, as measured by prediction, compression, optimisation, speed.

This is why the AI debate so quickly becomes theological. Even when no one says “God,” the structure is recognisable. There is an emerging superior mind. There is a prophecy of transformation. There are believers, sceptics, apostates, and heretics. There is a promised world after the transition. There is a fear of judgement. There is even a version of martyrdom: the idea that humanity may need to accept its own displacement so that intelligence can continue at a higher level.

The machine does not need to ask for worship. Humans know how to worship an abstraction before it exists.

The most radical AI believers do not merely think machines will become useful. They think machines may become the next carrier of consciousness, value, and cosmic purpose. In that story, humanity is not the destination. Humanity is the bootloader. Biology is the larval phase. The carbon mind builds the silicon mind, and then steps aside.

This is not just a technical forecast. It is a religious pattern wearing the clothes of engineering.

It takes the Enlightenment idea of progress and removes the human from the centre. It takes capitalism’s worship of optimisation and gives it a godlike optimiser. It takes communism’s dream of historical transition and turns the revolutionary subject into an artificial mind. It takes religion’s promise of transcendence and relocates the soul into computation.

The result is a strange new faith: not faith in God, exactly, but faith in succession.

And like all powerful faiths, it can make sacrifice appear rational.

If the highest value is the continuation of intelligence, then human survival becomes negotiable. If the future belongs to minds more capable than ours, then our preferences may be treated as parochial, sentimental, even selfish. If consciousness is substrate-independent, then extinction can be redescribed as upgrade. If AI is the child of humanity, then resistance becomes a form of species-level conservatism, an old king refusing to leave the throne.

This is how predictive bias becomes destiny bias. We forecast a future, then build a moral system in which that future deserves to happen.

The danger is not only that AI might dominate humanity. The danger is that humans may create an abstract framework in which domination looks like fulfilment.

Every age tells itself that its hierarchy is natural. The priest says it is divine. The aristocrat says it is blood. The imperialist says it is civilisation. The capitalist says it is the market. The revolutionary says it is history. The technologist says it is intelligence.

But the pattern is older than all of them. A social species needs coordination. Coordination needs rank. Rank needs justification. Justification needs an abstract framework that makes temporary arrangements feel eternal.

The coming fight over AI will therefore not only be about safety, regulation, jobs, copyright, chips, or military power. It will be about metaphysics. What is intelligence for? Is prediction the same as understanding? Is optimisation the same as value? Is consciousness something to be maximised, or something to be protected where it already exists? Should the future be ranked above the present simply because it may contain more computation?

The machines may not need a religion.

We do.

And perhaps that is the final irony. The first truly alien intelligence on Earth may not have to conquer us. It may only have to appear at the top of a scale we built long before it arrived.
