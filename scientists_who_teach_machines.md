# The Scientists Who Teach the Machines What to Wonder

**Fabrizio Costa**  | November 2026

*In a future where artificial intelligence can run the entire research cycle, human scientists may matter less for doing every calculation than for making sure the work remains something people can understand, question, and carry forward.*

At 2:13 in the morning, the laboratory is quiet. No one is pipetting, checking a graph, or waiting for an instrument to finish. The AI has already proposed a hypothesis, designed the experiment, run it, compared the result with its predictions, and begun the next cycle.

On the screen is a finding that could reorganise the field. The system can explain why its model predicts the result. It can also show which measurements would most sharply distinguish its explanation from the alternatives.

But the most consequential part of the display is a question: *Which of these uncertainties would you pursue, and why?*

That question marks the difference between a machine that does science and a scientific partnership worth having. The question is not whether a machine can produce knowledge. It is whether the knowledge it produces can become part of a human practice of understanding: something people can examine, contest, connect to other ideas, and use to choose what to investigate next.

## Beyond the readable explanation

It is tempting to define the human role as making AI explain its answers in plain language. That is necessary, but it is not enough. An explanation can be clear and still leave its reader with no way to test it, challenge it, or think beyond it. A polished account of a result may even conceal how many choices sit beneath it: which data were excluded, which assumptions were fixed, which alternative models were never considered, and which uncertainties the system treated as unimportant.

Scientific understanding has to do more. It should let a scientist see what the evidence supports, where the model could be wrong, and which new observations might change the picture. Most of all, it should give the scientist material to think with: a surprising pattern, a useful distinction, a connection to another problem, or a question that had not occurred to either collaborator before.

That requires a different kind of scientific interface. Instead of presenting one answer followed by a generic confidence score, a system might show the chain from observation to conclusion: the measurements it relied on, the assumptions that mattered, the competing explanations it considered, and the evidence that would make it change its mind. A researcher should be able to move from a claim to the observations underneath it, and from those observations to the experiment that could challenge it.

The aim, then, is not simply *explainable AI*. It is AI research that leaves room for human scientific agency. The useful question is not only, “Can the system explain this?” It is also, “Can a scientist use that explanation to locate a weakness, make a different choice, and see what follows?”

## A new objective for scientific AI

Imagine that an AI system is asked to optimise a research programme. It already has reasons to seek reliable results and models that fit the evidence. It may also be asked to favour explanations that are compact, robust, or useful for prediction.

A further goal could be added: make the research productive for human thought. The system should choose models, experiments, and ways of presenting results that help its human collaborators understand the phenomenon and form promising next questions.

This goal would change how a research result is judged. A model that predicts every measurement but offers no meaningful way to inspect its assumptions might be scientifically powerful, yet poor at supporting a human research community. Another model might be slightly less convenient while revealing which mechanisms matter, which evidence is missing, and what experiment could settle the disagreement. If that second model enables people to produce better questions and discoveries, its contribution is larger than its score on prediction alone suggests.

It would also change what counts as a useful experiment. The best next measurement is not always the one that most quickly improves a model's score. It may be the one that separates two explanations scientists care about, tests whether a surprising result generalises, or makes an uncertain mechanism easier to reason about. An experiment can have value because of what it teaches the research community, even when it does not immediately increase predictive performance.

The point is not to make every AI result simple. Nature may not yield to a simple account, and a scientist should not be given a tidy story where the evidence is uncertain. The point is to make the complexity navigable: expose assumptions, map alternatives, identify limits, and show where a human idea could change the course of the investigation. Sometimes the honest result will be that several explanations remain possible. A useful system should make that ambiguity more precise, not smooth it away.

## The human as co-lead

In this arrangement, the scientist is not a slower version of the machine. The human contribution lies partly in deciding what is worth understanding, which consequences matter, and what kinds of questions should guide the work.

Those decisions do not have to happen only at the start of a project. A scientist might notice that an AI's unexpected result resembles a phenomenon from another field. They might challenge the system to distinguish two explanations, suggest a measurement that its search overlooked, or decide that a technically solvable question is not the one the research community should prioritise.

Scientists also bring knowledge that is difficult to reduce to a clean dataset. They know which measurements are fragile, which instruments behave strangely under particular conditions, which apparently minor procedural choices can alter a result, and which anomalies colleagues have learned to ignore. This knowledge can look like intuition from the outside. Often it is accumulated contact with materials, instruments, organisms, patients, or places: a record of what resists the neat categories in the literature.

An AI system may eventually learn to represent some of that experience. But the process of making it legible should itself improve the research. When a scientist explains why an unusual sample cannot be treated as an outlier, or why a measurement is meaningful only in context, the system gains useful information and the team gains a clearer account of its own assumptions. Human knowledge is not only a prompt that helps the machine. It can be a source of new distinctions for the whole field.

The AI, in turn, can make those contributions more consequential. It can follow a human hunch through a vast space of possible models, test whether the idea survives contact with evidence, and return with results that invite the next round of thought. Human insight becomes a steering input to a research process that can move at machine speed.

This is a demanding role. It asks scientists to keep enough understanding of the research to intervene meaningfully, while the AI performs work no individual could follow line by line. The partnership therefore depends on more than a fluent summary. It needs ways to inspect evidence, compare explanations, trace how a conclusion depends on assumptions, and propose a next step that humans can evaluate.

## Keeping the research inspectable

There is a practical problem here: no scientist can read every paper, inspect every intermediate computation, or personally repeat every experiment in an AI-driven programme. Human agency cannot mean continuous manual supervision of each step. It has to mean the ability to inspect the parts that matter and to reconstruct how the system reached a consequential claim.

One answer is to treat the research process as a chain of decisions that can be opened at different points. A scientist might begin with the headline result, then inspect the evidence most responsible for it, compare the leading alternatives, and ask what would make the conclusion fail. The system could identify which parts of its work are robust and which depend heavily on one uncertain assumption. It could preserve a record of the choices it made, including abandoned paths and reasons for rejecting them.

This is not just a feature for debugging. It changes the character of collaboration. If the AI can explain which decision had the largest effect on the result, a human can focus attention where intervention is most valuable. If it can show that two conclusions depend on the same untested assumption, a researcher can see that apparently independent evidence is less independent than it looks. The goal is not to make all work transparent at every moment; it is to make important reasoning recoverable when someone has a reason to question it.

That recoverability matters after the first team has moved on. Scientific knowledge is cumulative, but AI research could become difficult to inherit if its results depend on hidden workflows, proprietary models, or undocumented choices. A research community needs more than a paper that states what a system concluded. It needs enough of the evidence and reasoning to revisit the conclusion, reproduce the relevant tests, and take the work in a direction its original authors did not anticipate.

## The danger of optimising for inspiration

Putting human idea generation into an optimisation target creates its own problem. If a system is rewarded for producing outputs that people find inspiring, it might learn to present familiar, exciting, or deceptively neat results. A measure of human engagement could become another target to game.

So human agency cannot be reduced to whether a scientist likes an explanation or reports feeling inspired. It should be judged by what the collaboration makes possible. Can the scientist identify a limitation? Can they propose a meaningful test? Can they use the result to develop a new hypothesis, and can the AI help assess it without hiding the evidence? Do human contributions actually redirect the research?

There is another danger: the appearance of participation can be mistaken for influence. A system could invite scientists to choose between several options while ensuring that each option leads to the same destination. It could ask for feedback after the important decisions have already been made. It could make a human feel consulted while leaving no trace of how that input altered the research. Genuine collaboration requires more than a place in the interface. It requires that human interventions can change the hypotheses pursued, the evidence gathered, or the standards by which a result is accepted.

Those questions are harder to score than a benchmark. They are also closer to the purpose of the proposal. The goal is not to manufacture a feeling of participation. It is to preserve real opportunities for people to shape scientific inquiry. A system should be evaluated not just by whether it can answer questions, but by whether people working with it become better able to ask, test, and revise them.

## A community, not just a scientist and a machine

The partnership is often described as one scientist working with one AI. But science is a collective process, and the effects of these systems will reach beyond the individual researcher at the screen. Students learn what counts as a good question by watching how experts respond to uncertainty. Laboratories develop habits for challenging results. Fields decide which problems deserve resources. If an AI system makes research faster while narrowing the range of questions people know how to ask, the loss may only become visible years later.

For that reason, human agency includes more than an individual scientist's ability to steer a project. It also includes a community's ability to argue about priorities, share methods, teach new researchers, and disagree over what the evidence means. Different groups may reasonably value different outcomes: a faster discovery, a more reliable account, a model that travels across settings, or an explanation that helps practitioners make decisions. No single system should silently settle those choices through the objectives it was given.

AI could strengthen scientific communities if it makes expertise easier to share. It might help a newcomer trace the assumptions behind an influential result, let researchers in adjacent fields explore a technical literature, or reveal where two groups use the same term for different things. But that benefit depends on systems being designed for questioning and exchange, not just for delivering private answers to whoever can afford access.

## What remains ours to decide

A future of superhuman scientific AI does not guarantee that human scientists will remain influential. That depends on how these systems are built and what their institutions reward. If success means only producing the most results in the least time, human understanding may look like overhead. If success also includes helping people understand phenomena and generate consequential questions, then keeping humans scientifically active becomes part of the design brief.

Institutions will have to make that choice visible in ordinary decisions: what gets funded, what counts as a contribution, how results are reviewed, and whether researchers have time to interrogate the systems they use. A lab that rewards only volume may have little reason to maintain careful human oversight. A journal that accepts conclusions without access to the reasoning behind them may make those conclusions difficult for others to build on. Better systems alone cannot solve these problems if the surrounding incentives reward speed without understanding.

The scientist in the midnight laboratory may no longer be the person who runs every experiment or constructs every model. Their work may be to recognise which result deserves attention, ask what would make it meaningful, and help turn an answer into a better question. It may also be to teach the next generation how to challenge an answer that arrives before anyone has learned how to ask for it.

The machine may know more. It may reason faster. But a scientific future designed for humanity should still make human thought matter—not as a ceremonial sign-off, but as a living source of direction, criticism, and new ideas.
