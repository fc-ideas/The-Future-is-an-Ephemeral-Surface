# The AI Art Debate Is Really About Where We Think Human Value Lives

**Fabrizio Costa**  | October 2026

*What do we value when we value art? AI brings that question into focus: the finished work, the choices that shaped it, the person behind it, and the culture through which we encounter it.*

A painting is never just a painting.

There is the object itself, of course: pigment on canvas, pixels on a screen, sound in a room. But there is also the person who made it, the tools they used, the decisions they made, the life they lived, the audience encountering the work, and the culture deciding whether any of this matters.

AI has made that structure suddenly visible.

For years, arguments about art have often collapsed these layers into a single question: Is the work good? Generative AI makes that question feel inadequate. Two images can look equally accomplished and yet provoke radically different reactions if one was painted over six months and the other appeared after a five word prompt.

That reaction is sometimes dismissed as nostalgia, gatekeeping, or fear of technology. But there is another possibility: people were never valuing the artifact alone.

They were valuing a network.

## Art has more than one object

Consider the simplest artistic system. There is an artist. There is an artwork. There is somebody experiencing it. There is a culture surrounding all three.

Now add the medium: brush, violin, camera, typewriter, synthesizer, Photoshop, Python, Midjourney.

Every pair of these things has a relationship.

The artist uses the tool. The artist produces the work. The audience encounters the work. The audience knows something about the artist. Society develops expectations about what art should look like. The artist accepts, ignores, or rebels against those expectations. Tools themselves arrive carrying histories, conventions, and assumptions about how they are supposed to be used.

And those relationships have properties.

The connection between an artist and an artwork might involve thousands of small decisions. A painter chooses pressure, direction, colour, speed, texture and placement. A photographer chooses framing, timing, lens, exposure, location and selection. A novelist makes decisions sentence by sentence.

Those decisions are not visible in the finished artifact in any simple way. Yet people often care about them.

This is one reason artistic value cannot be reduced to surface quality.

The process becomes part of the work.

## The strange importance of tiny decisions

This is where AI complicates things.

Imagine someone types:

> Make me a beautiful, original image.

A system generates one. The person accepts the first result.

Now imagine another artist spends three weeks working with the same model. They write code around it. They generate hundreds of variants. They manipulate latent representations, combine outputs, reject almost everything, edit details manually, deliberately exploit failure modes and eventually arrive at a final image.

Both used AI.

But almost nobody would describe the two processes as equivalent.

The difference is not simply effort. It is the density of meaningful intervention.

Artists traditionally exert control through enormous numbers of micro decisions. The hand moving a brush is one obvious example, but it is hardly unique. Editing film, programming a synthesizer, manipulating photographic chemistry or writing generative code can all involve similarly dense chains of choices.

AI therefore does not create a clean boundary between human art and machine art.

It creates a continuum.

At one end, the human specifies almost nothing and delegates almost everything. At the other, AI becomes one component inside a long, iterative process shaped by repeated human judgment.

Where somebody draws the line between those cases depends on what they value.

And that is precisely the point.

## Value belongs to observers

There is no single valuation system governing art.

The artist has one.

The individual encountering the work has another.

Society has another.

These systems overlap, but they need not agree.

An artist may believe that a work matters because of a highly specific technical experiment. An audience member may know nothing about that experiment and respond entirely to its emotional effect. A critic may care about its place in an artistic tradition. A collector may care about provenance. A community may value the artist's biography.

All of these judgments can attach to the same object.

This means the interesting unit is not simply the artwork. It is the artwork as seen through a particular system of values.

Take biography. People routinely assign artistic significance to the life of an artist: poverty, exile, illness, political persecution, obsession, sacrifice, reinvention. The knowledge changes the experience of the work.

Nothing physically changes in the canvas.

The network changes.

Information about the artist alters the relationship between audience and artwork, and value moves with it.

The same mechanism explains why an unsigned canvas discovered in an attic can change dramatically in cultural and monetary value after attribution to a famous painter. The object may remain identical. Its relational structure does not.

## Society is the slowest node in the network

Individual taste can change quickly. Cultural taste usually does not.

Society acts like a giant averaging mechanism. It contains institutions, habits, markets, museums, schools, critics, platforms, communities and accumulated history. Together they create a kind of cultural inertia.

That inertia matters because artistic innovation frequently begins by violating expectations.

A new work can initially appear wrong simply because the surrounding culture has not yet developed the categories needed to interpret it.

This creates a recurring pattern in art: deviation first appears as incompetence, ugliness or nonsense; later it may become a style.

The same tension now surrounds AI.

Some audiences treat extensive machine involvement as evidence that an artistic process has been weakened. Others see it as the emergence of a new medium whose conventions have barely begun to form.

Neither reaction exists independently of culture. They reflect different weighting schemes applied to the same relationships.

And those weights can move.

## Artists have always abused their tools

One of the most revealing features of artistic practice is that artists rarely behave like ideal users.

They misuse things.

They push film until it distorts. They overload amplifiers. They scratch negatives. They detune instruments. They exploit compression artifacts. They force software into states its designers never expected.

The history of creative technology is full of people discovering aesthetic possibilities inside technical failure.

That matters for AI because one of the strongest signals of artistic agency may eventually be the degree to which artists stop treating generative models as vending machines.

A tool becomes artistically interesting when somebody develops a practice around it.

That practice may involve controlling the tool. It may involve fighting it. It may involve discovering systematic failure modes and turning those failures into a visual language.

In other words, the artistic question is often not simply what the tool can do.

It is what this particular person learned to make the tool do.

That distinction survives the arrival of AI remarkably well.

## AI is less interesting as an artist than as a medium

Much of the debate starts from the wrong abstraction.

People ask whether AI is creative, whether AI is an artist, or whether machine output counts as art.

But generative AI can instead be placed inside the same structure as every other artistic technology.

Artist. Tool. Work.

Once framed that way, much of the apparent philosophical discontinuity disappears.

The useful questions become more concrete.

How much of the final structure was determined by the artist?

How many consequential choices did the artist make?

What did the artist reject?

What constraints did they invent?

Did they develop a recognisable method?

Did they push the medium into unfamiliar territory?

Did they use the system exactly as its designers intended, or discover something unexpected within it?

These questions do not automatically tell us whether something is good art.

They tell us what kind of artistic relationship exists.

And that may be enough.

## The artwork is only one node

The deeper lesson from generative AI may be that our old model of artistic value was always too object centred.

We talked as though art lived inside the thing.

But artistic value often emerges from a web of relations: between maker and object, object and audience, maker and tool, artist and society, society and audience, present and past.

Each relationship carries attributes. Each observer places different weights on those attributes. Those weights change over time.

The result is not chaos. It is a structured form of disagreement.

One person values virtuosity. Another values conceptual originality. Another values labour. Another values emotional force. Another values rebellion. Another values historical significance. Another cares only about what happens when they encounter the work.

AI does not resolve those disagreements.

It exposes them.

That may be why the argument feels unusually intense. Generative systems have compressed parts of artistic production that once took visible time and skill. In doing so, they have forced people to reveal what they thought they were valuing all along.

Was it the image?

The labour?

The intention?

The biography?

The struggle?

The originality?

The mastery of a medium?

The act of choosing?

The answer has probably always been: some weighted combination of all of them.

And everyone has been using different weights.
